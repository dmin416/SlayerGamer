# RoboCop — Detroit Gun Law

**Detroit, Michigan: Gun Laws, Carry Laws, and Self-Defense**

Legal overview as of November 11, 2026. Story reference for the RoboCop / Detroit campaign. Detroit follows Michigan's statewide firearm and self-defense laws. This is a legal-information overview, not case-specific legal advice.

Michigan recognizes open carry, licensed concealed carry, Stand Your Ground, and the Castle Doctrine, but each comes with important restrictions.

Michigan's laws distinguish between the right to possess a firearm, the right to carry it, and the right to use it. Legally possessing or carrying a handgun does not automatically justify drawing, pointing, or firing it.

## 1. Overview of rights

| Subject | Michigan / Detroit law |
|---|---|
| Own a handgun | Legal for eligible individuals |
| Open carry | Generally legal without a CPL if otherwise eligible |
| Concealed carry | Requires a concealed pistol license (CPL) or applicable exemption |
| Carry a loaded handgun in a vehicle | Generally requires a CPL or applicable exemption |
| Stand Your Ground | Yes, when statutory conditions are satisfied |
| Castle Doctrine | Yes, with a rebuttable presumption in certain intrusion cases |
| Defend another person | Yes, under the applicable self-defense standards |
| Deadly force to protect property alone | Generally not permitted |
| Fire warning shots | Not automatically justified and legally dangerous |
| Carry in schools and other restricted locations | Subject to significant restrictions |
| Background checks on firearm purchases | Required through Michigan's purchase licensing or exemption system |
| Safe storage | Specific legal requirements when minors are or likely will be present |

## 2. What makes Detroit different?

Michigan has a firearm-preemption law, **MCL 123.1102**, that substantially limits cities and counties from creating their own restrictions on firearm ownership, registration, purchases, transportation, and possession.

Consequently, Detroit generally cannot establish its own citywide prohibition on otherwise lawful handgun carry.

However, Michigan allows municipalities to regulate firearm discharge. Detroit has a local prohibition on discharging firearms, subject to exceptions and the operation of state self-defense law.

The practical distinction is that carrying a firearm lawfully in Detroit and discharging that firearm are separate legal questions.

The city firearm-discharge prohibition appears in the 2019 Detroit Code under **Section 31-13-25**. The current code references exceptions, and the exact ordinance should be consulted alongside applicable state law. A lawful self-defense claim and a violation of a city discharge restriction are not automatically resolved just because the individual had a license to carry.

## 3. Open carry

Michigan generally permits an eligible person to carry a legally possessed handgun openly without obtaining a concealed pistol license.

An openly carried handgun is ordinarily visible, such as a handgun carried in an exposed holster.

### Who can open carry?

- A Michigan resident age 18 or older may generally open carry a lawfully possessed handgun if not otherwise prohibited.
- A CPL is not generally required for ordinary open carry on foot.
- Carrying openly does not exempt someone from firearm-free-location restrictions.
- An openly carried pistol is subject to the same laws against threatening people, brandishing, and unlawful use of force.

The important exception is vehicles. Open carry on foot does not mean someone without a CPL may simply place a loaded handgun in a car and drive away.

Michigan's general concealed-weapon statute, **MCL 750.227**, addresses handguns in vehicles whether concealed or visible. An unlawful violation can be a felony carrying a maximum five-year prison sentence.

## 4. Concealed carry and the Michigan CPL

Michigan is not generally a permitless concealed-carry state.

A valid CPL authorizes a qualifying person to carry a pistol concealed on their person or in a vehicle, subject to restrictions.

### CPL eligibility

Michigan's standard requirements include:

- At least 21 years old.
- Michigan residency, ordinarily for six months before applying.
- Approved handgun safety training.
- No disqualifying felony conviction or pending felony charge.
- No disqualifying criminal, protective-order, or mental-health-related legal restrictions.
- Compliance with applicable federal firearm restrictions.

Michigan generally must issue a CPL to applicants who satisfy the statutory requirements, rather than requiring them to demonstrate a special personal need for protection.

### Applying for a CPL

1. Complete a qualifying pistol safety course.
2. Apply through the county clerk in the county of residence. Detroit residents generally use the Wayne County Clerk.
3. Submit the training certificate and application.
4. Pay the application fee and complete fingerprinting.
5. Pass the required background and eligibility checks.

| Item | Amount or period |
|---|---|
| Application and licensing fee | $100 |
| Fingerprinting | May involve an additional fee |
| Statutory processing period | 45 days after classifiable fingerprints are taken |

Under certain conditions, a fingerprinting receipt serves as a temporary CPL if the clerk has not issued either a license or a disqualification notice within the required 45-day period. That receipt has limitations, including for firearm purchases.

### Carrying with an out-of-state permit

Michigan recognizes a nonresident's valid concealed pistol license issued by their actual state of residence. The visitor must comply with Michigan's restrictions.

A permit from a state other than the person's state of residence does not automatically qualify for Michigan reciprocity.

## 5. Carrying firearms in vehicles

| Situation | General rule |
|---|---|
| Valid CPL holder with handgun | May generally carry in the vehicle, subject to legal restrictions |
| No CPL, loaded handgun in passenger compartment | Generally unlawful |
| No CPL, handgun visibly holstered while driving | Generally unlawful |
| No CPL, unloaded handgun transported in appropriate closed case in trunk | Generally lawful when statutory conditions are met |
| No CPL, handgun in a vehicle without a trunk | Must satisfy unloaded, cased, inaccessible transport requirements |
| Rifle or shotgun | Generally must be unloaded and meet statutory vehicle-transport conditions |

For ordinary handgun transportation without a CPL, **MCL 750.231a** provides exceptions for an unloaded, appropriately cased pistol in the trunk or, where there is no trunk, cased and not readily accessible to occupants. The firearm must be lawfully possessed and transported for a lawful purpose.

The vehicle exception does not turn a handgun into a lawfully carried defensive weapon inside the passenger compartment.

## 6. Places where carrying is restricted

Michigan has multiple overlapping laws rather than one universal list of prohibited places.

Under **MCL 28.425o**, ordinary CPL holders generally cannot carry a concealed handgun in:

- Schools and school property, subject to the specific vehicle exception for qualifying parent or guardian pickup and drop-off.
- Child-care facilities.
- Sports stadiums and arenas.
- Bars or taverns whose primary business is alcohol sold by the glass for on-site consumption.
- Religious facilities, unless the appropriate religious officials permit concealed carry.
- Entertainment facilities with known seating capacity of 2,500 or more.
- Hospitals.
- College or university dormitories and classrooms.

The statute ordinarily excludes parking areas from its definition of these premises. Casinos, court facilities, and certain federal properties have additional restrictions.

### Open carry has different rules

**MCL 750.234d** prohibits firearm possession in certain places, including banks, houses of worship, courts, theaters, arenas, day cares, hospitals, and liquor-licensed establishments, but has an exemption for licensed concealed-weapon carriers. That exemption does not override other applicable laws, court policies, or private-property rights.

### Election-related restrictions

Effective April 2, 2025, Michigan also restricted firearms in and around certain polling places, early-voting sites, ballot drop boxes, and election offices, including designated 100-foot areas and time periods. The statutory exceptions differ by location and include certain licensed concealed carry and lawful vehicle transport situations. Absent-voter counting places have a stricter separate rule.

## 7. Stand Your Ground: MCL 780.972

Michigan's Stand Your Ground protections come principally from the Self-Defense Act, Public Act 309 of 2006.

The law permits an individual to use qualifying defensive force without first retreating, including outside the home.

### When is deadly force legally justified?

For the statutory no-retreat protection to apply, the person must:

1. Not have been engaged in the commission of a crime at the time.
2. Be somewhere they have a legal right to be.
3. Honestly and reasonably believe deadly force is necessary to prevent imminent death, great bodily harm, or sexual assault against themselves or another person.

All three conditions matter.

The law does not require someone to wait until they have already been shot, stabbed, or seriously injured. It requires an honestly held and objectively reasonable belief in the specified imminent danger and the necessity of using deadly force.

### What "no duty to retreat" means

Imagine a person walking legally along a Detroit sidewalk who is suddenly confronted with an imminent threat of death or great bodily harm.

If the statutory conditions are met, Michigan does not require that person to attempt escape before using otherwise justified force.

The law does not mean that a person can use deadly force merely because they are frightened, insulted, or threatened in a vague way.

The absence of a duty to retreat does not eliminate the requirements of necessity, imminence, and reasonableness.

There is also an important distinction between having no legal obligation to retreat and having no reason to retreat. Safe withdrawal can still end a dangerous situation.

### Nondeadly force

The same statute permits nondeadly defensive force without retreat when an eligible person honestly and reasonably believes it necessary to prevent another person's imminent unlawful use of force.

A person being physically attacked may be justified in using reasonable nondeadly force to stop the attack, even when the aggressor's conduct does not present a deadly threat.

Deadly and nondeadly force are judged under different standards.

## 8. Castle Doctrine: MCL 780.951

Michigan's Castle Doctrine is an evidentiary presumption favoring a defender in specified intrusion situations. It is not unrestricted permission to use deadly force against any trespasser.

The statute creates a rebuttable presumption that the defender honestly and reasonably feared imminent death, sexual assault, or great bodily harm when the statutory circumstances exist.

### Where the protection applies

**Dwelling.** A person is breaking and entering, committing home invasion, or has done so and remains inside. The statutory definition of a dwelling includes places used temporarily as an abode.

**Business premises.** The statute also covers qualifying breaking and entering of business structures.

**Occupied vehicles.** The presumption covers an unlawful attempt to remove someone against their will from an occupied vehicle. It does not automatically cover every attempted vehicle break-in.

The defender must honestly and reasonably believe the intruder is engaging in the qualifying conduct.

### When the presumption does not apply

The statute specifies exceptions, including:

- A person with a lawful right to be present, such as an owner or lawful resident, subject to the statute's protective-order qualifications.
- Certain custody disputes involving removal of a child.
- A defender engaged in a crime or using the property to further a crime.
- An officer lawfully performing official duties.
- Certain domestic or former domestic relationships where the defender has a prior history of domestic violence as the aggressor.

The absence of the presumption does not necessarily mean all self-defense rights disappear. It means the person cannot rely on that particular statutory presumption and must establish the defense under whatever other applicable law permits.

### A home-invasion shooting is not automatically legal

A court may rebut the statutory presumption based on evidence. Investigators and prosecutors may still examine the circumstances, including whether the individual genuinely believed the conduct was an unlawful intrusion, whether the threat remained imminent, and whether a statutory exception applied.

The distinction between a violent forced entry and an uninvited person who is merely standing on someone's property can be legally decisive.

## 9. Defending property, family, and other people

Michigan also permits defense of others under **MCL 780.972**. The standard for deadly force focuses on the imminent danger facing the person being protected, not simply the defender's relationship with that person.

These examples illustrate the legal distinctions. They do not determine the outcome of any particular incident.

| Situation | Likely legal analysis |
|---|---|
| Someone forcibly enters an occupied home | Castle Doctrine presumption may apply |
| Someone threatens to kill another person and appears capable of doing so immediately | Deadly defense may be justified if genuinely necessary and reasonable |
| Someone punches another person during an argument | Nondeadly defense may be justified; deadly force needs a much stronger basis |
| Someone steals an unattended bicycle | Property theft alone generally does not justify deadly force |
| Someone breaks a car window to steal property | No automatic justification for deadly force merely to prevent the theft |
| Someone flees after stealing a wallet | Deadly force is generally not justified against a retreating thief absent an ongoing qualifying threat |
| Someone tries to forcibly abduct an occupant from a car | Castle Doctrine presumption may apply |
| Someone starts an assault with a deadly weapon | The aggressor's ability to claim self-defense is significantly limited |

### Initial aggressors

An individual who initiates a deadly assault generally cannot simply invoke Stand Your Ground against the predictable defensive response.

Michigan law also recognizes circumstances in which an initial aggressor withdraws from an altercation and clearly communicates that withdrawal. The exact facts affect whether a later claim of self-defense is available.

In a criminal case where there is sufficient evidence supporting lawful self-defense, the prosecution bears the burden of disproving the defense beyond a reasonable doubt.

## 10. Drawing, pointing, and brandishing a gun

Michigan law distinguishes carrying a visible firearm from deliberately displaying it in a threatening manner.

Under **MCL 750.234e**, knowing and willful public brandishing is generally a misdemeanor, but the statute expressly recognizes lawful self-defense or defense of others as an exception.

Pointing a gun can have different legal consequences from firing one. Michigan case law and jury instructions distinguish between the use of deadly force and threatening display, but pointing a firearm can still constitute a serious criminal offense depending on the circumstances.

A person cannot assume that drawing a firearm during an argument is legal simply because they possess a CPL.

## 11. Civil liability and criminal consequences

Michigan provides a specific civil-liability protection under **MCL 600.2922b**.

When a person uses force in compliance with the Self-Defense Act, that person is immune from certain civil damages claims by the individual against whom force was authorized and by people asserting related injury or death claims based on their relationship to that individual.

This is not blanket immunity from investigation, prosecution, or every possible lawsuit.

It also does not erase unrelated firearm offenses. Someone who unlawfully carried a pistol before an incident may face a separate legal question from whether a particular defensive use of force was justified.

## 12. Buying and owning firearms

Michigan's firearm purchase rules changed substantially on February 13, 2024.

### Minimum ages

| Transaction | General minimum age |
|---|---|
| Handgun from federally licensed dealer | 21 |
| Handgun through qualifying private transfer | 18 |
| Rifle or shotgun from licensed dealer | 18 |
| Michigan CPL | 21 |

The purchaser must also satisfy state and federal eligibility requirements.

### Background checks and purchase licenses

Michigan requires firearm purchase licensing or a statutory exemption for qualifying transactions, including private transfers.

The rules depend on the firearm and purchaser:

- A purchaser without a CPL generally needs the applicable License to Purchase (LTP), unless another statutory exception applies.
- A CPL holder has an exemption from the ordinary purchase-license requirement, subject to specified conditions.
- For non-pistol purchases through federally licensed dealers, a recent qualifying federal background check can satisfy an exception.
- Michigan maintains pistol transaction records. The seller generally must submit the required pistol transaction documentation to the designated law-enforcement agency within ten days.

An LTP generally must be used within 30 days after issuance. Michigan's purchase-licensing system is distinct from the CPL system.

### People prohibited from possessing firearms

Michigan law includes firearm restrictions for certain felony convictions, domestic-violence misdemeanor convictions, and qualifying court orders.

Under **MCL 750.224f**, certain felony restrictions last three or five years after specified sentence obligations are completed, with additional restoration requirements in some cases. Michigan also establishes an eight-year prohibition after completion of the specified sentence obligations for qualifying domestic-violence misdemeanors.

Federal prohibitions can last longer, so expiration of a Michigan restriction does not necessarily restore federal firearm rights.

## 13. Safe storage and Michigan's red-flag law

### Safe storage: MCL 28.429

When a firearm is stored or left unattended somewhere a person controls, and that person knows or reasonably should know that a minor is or is likely to be present, the statute requires appropriate securing of the firearm.

The two principal statutory options are a locked container or keeping the firearm unloaded and secured with an appropriate locking device.

Criminal penalties depend on the circumstances and consequences of a minor obtaining the improperly stored firearm. These are statutory maximums in the specified circumstances, not automatic sentences. Exceptions and causation requirements apply.

| Consequence | Maximum imprisonment |
|---|---|
| Minor publicly exhibits or recklessly possesses it | 93 days |
| Discharge causes injury | 5 years |
| Serious bodily impairment | 10 years |
| Discharge causes death | 15 years |

### Extreme Risk Protection Orders (ERPOs)

Michigan also permits courts to issue orders temporarily prohibiting firearm possession and purchase when the statutory risk criteria are met.

These orders can last up to one year and can be extended. A person subject to an order has procedures for requesting a hearing and seeking termination of the order.

A CPL does not override an active ERPO.

## 14. Important rules while carrying

### Police encounters

Under **MCL 28.425f**, a CPL holder carrying a concealed pistol must carry their CPL and government-issued identification, and produce them when lawfully requested.

When stopped by a peace officer while carrying a concealed pistol, the CPL holder must immediately disclose the concealed firearm.

Failure to disclose can result in a $500 civil fine and a six-month CPL suspension for a first offense, with greater consequences for qualifying subsequent violations.

### Alcohol and firearms

Michigan has particularly strict rules for concealed carry and alcohol. Actual intoxication or controlled-substance impairment can also trigger violations regardless of the numeric alcohol threshold. Separate laws restrict firearm possession and use while impaired.

| Blood alcohol content | CPL consequence under MCL 28.425k |
|---|---|
| 0.02 to below 0.08 | Civil infraction; one-year suspension |
| 0.08 to below 0.10 | Misdemeanor; three-year suspension |
| 0.10 or higher | Misdemeanor; CPL revocation |

### Private businesses and property

State permission to carry does not create a right to remain on private property against its owner's wishes.

If a business prohibits firearms, the specific legal consequences depend on the premises, applicable statutes, and whether someone refuses a lawful instruction to leave. Signs should not be treated as the only relevant legal issue.

## 15. Additional Detroit-specific considerations

See Section 2 for the Detroit discharge ordinance, **Section 31-13-25**.

Michigan State Police published guidance in October 2026 regarding short-barreled rifles and shotguns, explaining that their treatment under Michigan law remains connected to whether the weapons are lawful under federal law. This is an area where the firearm's classification matters.

## 16. Practical self-defense scenarios

These are general illustrations, not definitive determinations of criminal or civil liability.

| Scenario | Most relevant legal consideration |
|---|---|
| Armed intruder forcibly enters your home | Castle Doctrine's rebuttable presumption |
| Armed attacker threatens you on the street | Stand Your Ground, imminence, necessity |
| Someone threatens your spouse or child with deadly force | Defense of others |
| Unarmed person steals property from your porch | Property theft alone generally insufficient for deadly force |
| Someone attacks you but then retreats | Whether any imminent threat remains |
| A driver threatens you during road rage | Same legal standard of imminent serious danger; a disagreement alone is insufficient |
| Person tries to forcibly remove you from your occupied vehicle | Specific Castle Doctrine presumption may apply |
| You carry concealed for protection without a CPL | Stand Your Ground does not generally excuse the separate carry offense |

After a serious self-defense incident, contacting emergency services, obtaining appropriate medical assistance, and seeking qualified legal representation are important. A claim of self-defense does not guarantee that police will decline to investigate or that charges will never be filed.

## Bottom line

Michigan's Stand Your Ground and Castle Doctrine laws provide substantial self-defense protections, including situations outside the home. They are not permission to use deadly force merely because a person is trespassing, stealing property, behaving suspiciously, or refusing to leave. The legal focus remains on the circumstances of the threat, the necessity of the response, and the specific statutory requirements.

Detroit residents should use the current statutes and consult a Michigan firearms attorney for decisions involving actual carry practices or a particular defensive incident.
